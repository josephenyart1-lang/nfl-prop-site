Legs

What every leg has to clear

An entry pays only if all of it lands, so the bar is per leg. A single bet at a sportsbook needs 52.4%.

The payout is not fixed by how many picks you make: it moves with the picks' own odds, so two five-pick entries can pay differently. This page used to assume 20x for five picks and publish the bar that followed. A real five-pick paid 8.25x, where the bar is 65.6% rather than 54.9%. So read the payout off your own entry screen and find its row below. A row marked in colour is one actually seen.

Measured, not claimed —

Results

Two different things get called results, and mixing them up is how a model gets oversold. Accuracy is how close the predictions land. The record is whether the side the model actually took came in. Only the second could show an edge.

The record

Every prop on the board with a real line is recorded when it is put up, and graded once the game is played. Nothing is staked and no odds are shopped, so there is no profit here to report — only whether the call landed. A prop Underdog voided, because the player did not play enough, counts as neither right nor wrong; voids are shown rather than dropped, since a record that hides them is one you could improve by finding more.

Read the claim, not the tally. Over a few dozen props a run of luck swamps everything, but the claimed probabilities should still come out right: if the model says 57% twenty times, about eleven should land. That is what the last tile compares. Below 30 settled props none of this means much.

The claim has not held up the same way on both sides of the line.

Are the stated chances honest against a line it did not choose?

Landing close is one thing; quoting an honest price is another. A model that says 70% should be right about seven times in ten. Every game in the test seasons was priced against a spread of made-up lines above and below its own prediction, which sweeps the whole range from near-certain to near-hopeless, and the claims were then sorted into bands and checked against what actually happened. On the diagonal is perfect; below it the model is overconfident. What this cannot test is the only situation the board is ever in — a line somebody else set, and a side taken because the model disagrees with it. The record above is the one measurement that sees that, which is why the two can disagree.

How it works

Method

Train on the past, test on the future. Decisions are made by predicting each season from the seasons before it alone. 2024-2026 is read once, at the end, and never used to choose anything. No split is random.

Volume splits into role and pace. A receiver's share of his team's targets is stable; his team's passing volume is not. Share correlates 0.72 with his next game, against 0.24 for a quarterback's attempts and 0.76 for a back's share of carries. So share and volume are predicted apart and multiplied.

Probabilities come from real misses, not a bell curve. The model's errors lean right: a quarterback can throw twenty more than expected chasing a game, but not twenty fewer without getting hurt. A normal curve would price both tails wrong, so the actual distribution of past misses is used instead.

The quiet games count now. A pass-catcher who played and was never thrown to used to have no row at all, so the model was fitted and scored only on afternoons where somebody was targeted. It lifted the quiet end as a result, over-predicting a man it put at two catches or fewer by about three quarters of a catch. Those games are carried now — 12,453 of them, each one proved by a snap count or by a carry, because a man who ran the ball was on the field whatever the snap file says — which makes the table above both larger and harder: targets went from 6,879 games to 7,100 as marginal players became predictable at all. So the model's own error rises a little on the bigger exam while its margin over a player's recent average widens. On identical rows it is clearly better.

The same hole on the other side of the ball. A back who played and never carried had no row either, and that mattered twice: the carry model never met the quiet end, and the estimate of whether a back carries AT ALL — which goes straight onto every carries price — was fitted on a pool where everyone had played. 1,949 of those games are carried now. The carry number barely moves; what moves is that chance, whose useful range widens from 0.66-0.91 out to 0.48-0.99, so a near-certain starter and a rotational back are no longer told apart by almost nothing.

What it cannot see. Not a coach's intentions: a team's own run-pass mix over its last eight games barely predicts its next, correlating 0.29 across 4,638 team-games and missing the real rate by eight points of it. And the board is priced days before kickoff and not looked at again, so a Saturday ruling can land after a Thursday price. It does read the weekly injury report, the depth chart and snap counts, and a player his team has ruled out is kept off the board altogether. What it misses is a role that has already gone: a back who takes the field and never touches the ball leaves no row in the table the role signal reads, so the week his job disappeared is a week that never happened. That is how it priced Pierre Strong at 8.3 carries against a line of 1.5 and watched him take 1 — it was reading his one game as the lead back as though it were his latest. The cause is understood and the fix is not: taking those two numbers off the snap counts instead was measured on both horizons and came out worse. And a loud disagreement is so far neither a warning nor a recommendation: there have been too few either way to lean on.

A lean it still has. On pass attempts the model claims a shade too much on the over: through the middle of the chart above, a stated 45% happens about 43% of the time. That was measured over 26,975 priced cases and chased down. It is not the prediction, which runs only a third of an attempt high, and it cannot be fixed by nudging the prediction down — the pool of past misses re-centres by exactly as much, so the probability does not move. It comes from the middle of that pool sitting about a third of an attempt above the misses these seasons really produced. Using every season of misses in hand, rather than a pool frozen in 2023, cut the lean by more than half. The rest of it needs something no one knows before a season starts.

What it has not shown. That it beats a sportsbook. Against a bookmaker's own implied probabilities it has priced two pass-attempt wagers, about seven points above the book on both, and neither has been graded. That is a disagreement about price, not a result. On the other markets there are only a handful of priced cards so far either. Nothing here is a recommendation to bet. If you do, risk only what you can lose.
